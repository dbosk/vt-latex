\section{Semantic markup}
\label{sec:markup}

The third educational objective is markup as \emph{meaning}: sections,
emphasis, quotations, cross-references and citations declare what things
\emph{are}; how they \emph{look} is decided elsewhere.

\subsection{Documented difficulties}

The systematic round (\cref{app:protocol}) found no studies of learners'
understanding of semantic versus presentational markup---and the gap is
not peculiar to LaTeX.
A PRISMA review of teaching web design finds empirical research on
teaching even HTML and CSS---markup taught far more widely than
LaTeX---\enquote{scarce} and in a \enquote{nascent state}, the few studies
reporting difficulties with \enquote{complex material comprehension}
\autocite{Kharchenko2025WebDesign}; the misconception of markup as
\emph{meaning} rather than appearance is, as far as we can tell, nowhere
documented empirically.
Authoring a structured document with markup is a form of end-user
programming, for which a canonical taxonomy identifies learning barriers
beyond the language itself---among them \emph{understanding} and
\emph{use} barriers \autocite{Ko2004Barriers}; we conjecture that the leap
from \enquote{make it look right} to \enquote{say what it is} is such an
understanding barrier for LaTeX learners.
The round that looked specifically for it
(\cref{sec:protocol-measurement}) found no study of learners'
understanding of semantic versus presentational markup, of word-processor
styles versus direct formatting, or of cross-references and numbering;
the separation of content from presentation appears in the
web-development literature only as one of the computational concepts a
course exercises, not as a documented difficulty \autocite{Park2011WebDev}.
Our own course data (the datintro LaTeX lab grading specifically checks
semantic-markup use) supplies the local observations
(cf.\ \cref{sec:method-course-data}).

\subsection{Candidate critical aspects}

% XXX Preliminary.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Say what it is, not how it looks}: \verb|\section| versus
    large bold text; \verb|\emph| versus italics.
  \item \emph{One declaration, many appearances}: the same source
    renders differently under a different class or style.
  \item \emph{References are relations}: \verb|\ref|/\verb|\cite| link to
    things, and the compiler maintains the links.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Patterns of variation}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Contrast (markup varies, appearance invariant): a hand-formatted
    \enquote{heading} (bold, large) next to \verb|\section|---identical
    look, then observe the table of contents and numbering diverge.
  \item Contrast (style varies, markup invariant): compile the same
    semantically marked-up source under two document classes.
  \item Fusion: renumber by inserting a section early in the
    document---automatic numbering, references and table of contents all
    update.
\end{itemize}
